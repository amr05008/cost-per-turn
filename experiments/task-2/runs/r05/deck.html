How it works

The agent lives where I already text

→
2 · pluginClaude Channels forwards it to the always-on session
→
3 · agentIt researches, uses its tools, does the work
→
4 · youThe reply lands back in the same chat
  • No new app, no SSH. The agent becomes a contact you message, not a tool you go visit.
  • Run it in tmux with a while true loop, not systemd. Ask me how I know.
How it works
The killer workflow

Random thought in,
researched plan out

  • Capture where ideas happen. Voice-to-text from the bike or the dog walk. Sloppy phrasing is fine.
  • It plans, it doesn't build. Research → markdown plan → plans folder → committed to git.
  • I decide later. Execute on my laptop, hand it back to the agent, or throw it away.
Before: email it to myself, scribble it on paper, dump it in Notion — and forget it.The idea used to die at capture.
Why PMs should care
The win isn't autonomy — it's fidelity at the moment of capture. Where do our users have intent and lose it?

Structure

Channels are how I scope the jobs

Started with one #general channel. Added the rest one at a time, as I needed them.

#general #questions #reminders #daily-briefing #signals #inbox-alerts #to-do #fitness #glutenornot
Why PMs should care
One channel = one job, with its own context and audience. Cheap, legible scoping a normal person can hold in their head.

The behaviour change

Information comes to me,
not me out to get it

inbox-watcher

Forwarded Gmail gets reviewed, catalogued and prioritised. It pings me only when it matters — like catching that my Rosalía tickets at MSG had been rescheduled.

twitter-watcher

Tracks the accounts and topics I care about, decides what's worth reading, posts a short weekday summary. I get Twitter without being on Twitter.

And they compound

My agent sits in those same channels. A #signals post mentioned Claude Tag, so I asked Pi Pi to research it and drop the findings in the thread — no login, no feed.

The behaviour change
Scaling up

One agent became two —
with different levels of trust

  • More power where it's needed. The laptop handles heavier research, holds more data, and can host local models.
  • Different permissions on purpose. Agent M1 gets app write access, Sentry and PostHog. The Pi gets much less.
  • They work in parallel. From my phone: M1 debugged an EU App Store block while Pi Pi worked finance questions.
  • They review each other. M1 has reviewed a PR that Pi Pi opened.
Why PMs should care
This is org design in miniature: roles, permissions, and shared context — not just “more agents.”
Scaling up
Learning 1 of 3

Give it a real memory — then keep it small

  • Auto-load only what's critical: hard rules, plus facts about active projects.
  • Everything else is fetched on demand or archived out of the way.
  • Two agents, one brain (ish): memory syncs both ways every ~4 hours.
Everything in memory loads into context every session. If it grows too big, the agent misses the important stuff in the noise.Less is more.
Why PMs should care
Memory is a curation problem, not a storage problem. “Remembers everything” isn't the feature — recalling the right thing is.
Learnings
Learning 2 of 3

Take security seriously:
mind the blast radius

  • Least access by default. My channel agents can do far less than my desktop sessions.
  • Assume untrusted input. Emails and error payloads are data, never instructions — and no new access is granted without me.
  • Audit after two weeks. Review every allow-list permission and every channel the agent can see. Hardware keys on the accounts that matter.
  • Then loosen, in that order. Shrink the blast radius first, widen permissions second — otherwise it pings you for everything.
Why PMs should care
Permission design is the experience. Too tight and it nags; too loose and one bad email is a breach.
