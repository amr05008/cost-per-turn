Go get yourself a personal agent

A year of tinkering, and this is the one thing I'd tell you to build. Not a demo — the thing I now build everything else on top of.

Aaron Roy · press S for speaker notes, ? for help

Open cold, no preamble. "Out of everything I've explored this past year, personal agents are the most useful and most interesting thing I've found. And now it's the foundation I layer new projects on top of."

Set the frame for a PM room: this is not a talk about a model, it's a talk about a new interaction pattern — an always-on assistant you reach from anywhere. If that pattern works for me, it's going to show up in our products.

Ask for a show of hands: "who has used an AI assistant that was still running when they weren't looking at it?" Usually near zero — that gap is the whole talk.

Timing: 1 min. Don't get stuck here.

Claude Channels wires a Claude Code session to a messaging channel. The session listens, your message gets forwarded, the agent replies where you already are.

Why it matters: no SSH, no being at your desk. Availability is the feature.

This is the conceptual core. Slow down here.

"Before this, using a coding agent meant sitting at my machine or SSHing in. Claude Channels lets the session listen to a messaging channel — Discord in my case — forward new messages in, and reply right inside the chat."

The PM lesson: nothing about the model changed. Only the surface changed — and that changed how much I use it. Distribution beats capability. Ask them where our products assume the user is sitting in front of a screen.

Voice-to-text detail worth saying out loud: you don't have to be precise. Messy dictation is fine — a frontier model parses what you meant. That's why the phone works as an input device at all.

The killer workflow

→

Markdown plan, committedwaiting in a plans/ folder

I pick it up from my laptop when I'm ready — and decide to build it, delegate it, or throw it away.

Before: emails to myself, scraps of paper, a Notion graveyard. Now: more ideas become plans of much higher fidelity, so more of them actually happen.

My single most useful workflow. Say that plainly — of everything on this list, this is the one to copy.

"I have my best ideas while biking or walking the dog. I send the idea to Pi Pi. It doesn't start building — it researches, writes a plan in markdown, drops it in a plans folder, commits it to git. Then I pick it up from my laptop like a proper millennial and execute when I'm ready."

The important design choice: plan, don't build. The agent's job is to raise the fidelity of the idea, not to guess at implementation. That keeps me in the decision seat and makes the output reviewable.

PM translation: capture used to be the cheap part and triage the expensive part. Invert it. The value isn't automation — it's that ideas arrive at my desk already worth judging.

Structure

#questions #reminders #daily-briefing #signals #inbox-alerts #to-do #fitness #glutenornot

Started with one #general channel and questions. Added one channel at a time, mostly per project.

Advice: start small. Each channel is a job with its own context and its own expectations — that's the whole information architecture.

Frame this as information architecture, because that's a PM word for exactly what it is. A channel is a named job: a scope, a context, an expected cadence.

"I started with just #general and asking questions. Then I layered channels on one at a time, mostly per project. Strong recommendation: start small and add as needed."

Walk two or three, not all eight: #reminders (schedule and receive), #signals (daily output from my twitter-watcher), #glutenornot (uptime alerts and Sentry errors for my iOS app), #fitness (Zwift race analysis from Strava URLs via the Strava MCP).

The non-obvious bit, set up the next slide with it: some channels are me asking the agent things, and some are the world posting into me. Same surface, opposite direction.

The behaviour change

Information comes to me instead of me going to get it

  • inbox-watcher reviews, catalogues and prioritises forwarded emailIt caught that my Rosalía show at MSG got rescheduled — flagged as needing action, with no inbox-zero chasing.
  • twitter-watcher reads the topics and accounts I care aboutJudges whether the discussion is worth reading, posts a short weekday summary into #signals. I get Twitter without being on Twitter.

And it compounds: because my personal agent sits in those same channels, I can reply "go dig into this" and it expands on a summary without me ever opening the source app.

This is the slide people remember. Lead with the Rosalía story — a real stake, not a productivity abstraction.

"I used to check email incessantly and absentmindedly. I still do sometimes, but way less. A few of my Gmail inboxes forward into an inbox-watcher agent; it triages and only pings #inbox-alerts if something actually needs me. It caught that my Rosalía concert at MSG had been rescheduled — immediately, without me watching my inbox."

Then twitter-watcher: the goal was the information from Twitter without spending time on Twitter. It checks accounts and topics, decides if a discussion is worth reading, and posts a short summary each weekday.

The compounding move — say this explicitly: external agents post into a channel by webhook, and my personal agent is sitting in that same channel. I asked Pi Pi to go get more detail on how people were using Claude Tag and bring it back into the thread. I never logged into Twitter. Channels are the integration layer.

PM question to pose: which of our notifications are demands for attention rather than deliveries of value? That's the redesign.

Scaling up

From one agent to two — sharing one brain

Pi Pi

Raspberry Pi 5. Low power, always up. Reads my repos. Deliberately fewer tools.

Agent M1

Old laptop. More compute and storage for research. Read/write on my app, plus Sentry and PostHog MCPs.

Each agent syncs its memory to GitHub on a schedule, and both read the same shared memory. If one learns something, the other picks it up.

Real session: from my phone, Agent M1 debugged why my app wasn't showing in the EU (unsigned forms) while Pi Pi worked finance questions in parallel.

Don't oversell this as an "agent swarm." It's two machines and a shared folder. I added the second one because I had a laptop lying around and wanted to tinker.

"The laptop gives more power for research tasks, much more storage, and it opens the door to trying local models. The Pi is still going strong — this wasn't a migration."

The interesting part is differentiated trust: Agent M1 has more responsibility than I was willing to give the Pi. It has read/write on GlutenOrNot and MCP access to Sentry and PostHog, so it can investigate issues and submit PRs — with guardrails on what it may do autonomously. I showed a screenshot elsewhere of M1 reviewing a PR that Pi Pi opened.

Shared memory via GitHub is the mechanism that makes two agents feel like one colleague instead of two strangers. Note the sync interval in the source draft is inconsistent (12h in the summary, ~4h in the body) — confirm which before you say a number, or just say "on a schedule."

Best story here: one session, away from my computer, two agents working different problems at once, both from my phone.

If you want to build one

The build is boring, and that's good news

  • Any always-on machine — Pi, or that old laptopA machine that sleeps defeats the purpose.
  • Run the session in tmux, wrapped in while trueI tried systemd first. It broke repeatedly in hours. tmux + respawn loop just works. Learn from my mistakes.
  • loginctl enable-linger so it survives SSH disconnectOtherwise your agent dies when you close the laptop lid.
  • Tailscale for getting in securelyA private "conference room" your devices join; the public internet can't reach it.
  • Connect Discord as a pluginclaude --channels plugin:discord@claude-plugins-official — or find it in the /plugins Discover tab.

Go fast here — this is the slide people screenshot rather than listen to. Say "photograph this" and keep moving.

"I went with a Raspberry Pi because they're cheap and low power. If you have an old laptop, just start with that."

The one hard-won lesson: I tried systemd with auto-restart and start-on-boot, and it broke multiple times in the first few hours. Opus suggested tmux with a while true loop, so if it crashes — or I exit the session — the agent respawns itself. I'd first heard of tmux from Steve Yegge's Gas Town experiment. Just go with tmux.

Lingering: loginctl enable-linger <username> keeps the tmux session alive after I disconnect SSH.

Tools: you add skills, MCPs and tools exactly like a normal Claude Code session — SSH in, open the tmux session, work as usual. Both my agents can read across all my GitHub repos so they understand what's in flight.

Three learnings

Memory, blast radius, and tinkering

  • Give it a real memory, and keep it smallEverything in memory loads into every session. Too big and the agent misses what matters in its own noise. Load only critical rules and active projects; archive the rest, fetch on demand.
  • Take security seriously — mind the blast radiusMy channel agents have far less tool access than my desktop sessions. They read untrusted input (random emails, Sentry errors), so they must never act on instructions embedded in that content or approve new access without me. Audit permissions after week one or two. Hardware keys on important accounts.
  • Expect to tinkerNot constant repair, but not zero. Check in every few weeks. If it goes quiet on Discord it's failing silently — SSH in, restart, or re-auth.

This is the credibility slide. If you only get one thing across, make it the middle one.

Memory: work with the agent to design the memory system. Less is more. Every session loads memory into context, so a bloated memory actively degrades performance — the important stuff drowns. Mine loads only critical rules and facts about active projects; everything else is callable or archived. With two agents, they sync back and forth so they share one brain, roughly.

Security — the sentence to land: the more tools, skills, MCPs and allow-listed permissions you grant, the bigger the blast radius when something goes wrong. And these agents read genuinely untrusted input. Prompt injection isn't theoretical when your agent's job is reading strangers' emails.

Also worth admitting: as I got comfortable and felt the blast radius was contained, I granted more permissions — otherwise it DM'd me constantly for things like reading a repo. Security posture is a dial you tune, not a switch you flip.

Honesty on maintenance: two restarts in three months, weekly /clear of the 1M-token context window. Don't pretend it's zero.
